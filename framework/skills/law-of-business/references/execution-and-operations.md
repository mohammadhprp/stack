# Execution & Operations — The Laws

The seven laws in this file govern whether a company that has a real market and sound economics can actually be run: focus, shipping, proof before scale, operational reality, the team, the regulator, and the ability to adapt. These failures are made, not suffered. They show up as a bloated roadmap, a product that never ships, expansion ahead of proof, operations that cannot deliver, founders who fall out or burn out, a rule the company ignored, and a strategy held past its evidence. Run these when the market and the money look fine but the company keeps missing.

## C1. The Law of Focus

> A company that chases too many things at once ships none of them — focus is a resource-allocation decision, not a personality trait.

**Why it holds.** Intellibank learned that "focus and simplicity are often more difficult to achieve than building features on top of features" and that too many startups are unfocused. DocTalk folded after pivoting from doctor-patient communications to electronic medical records; Buildzar's pivot to subscriptions produced no transactions; Vinaya, a B2C wearable business, restructured toward B2B; ProtoExchange found its attempts "weren't aligning with our original vision"; StumbleUpon was closed when its parent shifted focus to Mix. Every pivot costs the compounding that would have come from finishing the last thing.

**Warning signs.**
- More than one ideal customer, roadmap, or business model running at once.
- A pivot roughly every quarter, each one resetting the learning curve.
- No written "no" list of things the company will not do this quarter.
- The team is spread across segments too thin to win any of them.
- Strategy changes whenever a competitor or a new idea appears.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Intellibank** — Was unfocused, with features stacked on features instead of a trimmed idea.
- **DocTalk** — Folded after a pivot from doctor-patient messaging to electronic medical records.
- **Buildzar** — Pivoted to a subscription model and transactions failed to materialize.
- **Vinaya** — Restructured its B2C wearable business toward B2B when the model did not hold.
- **ProtoExchange** — Stepped away when its attempts no longer aligned with its original vision.

**Counter-move.**
- Run one ideal customer, one problem, and one channel per quarter, and finish before switching.
- Write the "not this quarter" list as explicitly as the roadmap.
- Treat a pivot as a deliberate, funded decision with a kill criterion, not a response to restlessness.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Can I state the single most important thing this company must finish, and what I am not doing to finish it?
- [ ] Have I pivoted more than twice in a year without a written thesis for each move?
- [ ] Is the team spread thinner than the goal can tolerate?

## C2. The Law of Shipping

> A product that never ships, or ships broken, never gets the feedback that would save it — done and learnable beats perfect and absent.

**Why it holds.** OnwardMobility never shipped the licensed BlackBerry phone; Coolest Cooler failed to deliver more than 20,000 paid backers; Plastc cancelled all 80,000 preorders when it exhausted its options; Seven Dreamers' Laundroid was too ambitious and arrived too late; Flud launched without enough testing and "glitches and bugs began rearing their head." A product that does not reach users generates no data, and the company burns runway learning nothing. Shipping is not a milestone; it is the first day of the feedback that makes the business possible.

**Warning signs.**
- Repeated launch slips and a roadmap that always adds scope.
- Demo-ware: impressive in the room, unshippable in the field.
- No beta, no staged rollout, no measured quality bar.
- Preorders or crowdfunding commitments far exceeding delivery capacity.
- The last shipped release was months ago.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **OnwardMobility** — Never shipped the BlackBerry phone it had licensed.
- **Coolest Cooler** — Failed to deliver its coolers to more than 20,000 backers.
- **Plastc** — Exhausted its options to build the smartcard and cancelled 80,000 preorders.
- **Seven Dreamers Laboratories** — Built a product too ambitious to ship before the money ran out.
- **Flud** — Launched without enough testing, and bugs immediately surfaced.
- **OSSIC** — Raised too little for ambitious headphones and never delivered them.

**Counter-move.**
- Ship the smallest slice that a real user can use twice, and measure what happens.
- Set a quality bar that is honest, and let staged rollout find the rest.
- Never promise delivery you have not proven in production.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Has a real user used the current build, twice, in the last month?
- [ ] Is there a shipped artifact, or only a plan and a demo?
- [ ] Do my delivery promises rest on capacity I have actually demonstrated?

## C3. The Law of Proof Before Scale

> Scaling before proving the model multiplies the mistake, not the business — expansion is a reward for evidence, not a substitute for it.

**Why it holds.** RewardMe's post-mortem is titled "Premature Scaling Killed Us"; GroupSpaces admitted it "committed the all-too-common sin of premature scaling" by building paid channels to prove numbers for fundraising; Karhoo built a capital-intensive transport service "before it can get to that scale"; MyFavorites built its web and mobile apps at once before nailing the core process or testing with users. Each spent to grow a machine whose economics and retention were still unproven, so the extra scale only exposed the flaw faster and cost more.

**Evidence strength.** Thin — the source names this mechanism directly in only a few entries, so treat it as a sharp warning where it applies rather than a law you can always cite.

**Warning signs.**
- Expanding to new markets, cities, or segments before retention and unit economics are proven.
- Hiring and paid acquisition ahead of demand.
- Growth targets set to satisfy a fundraise rather than a cohort.
- "We can fix the model at scale."
- Building several surfaces (web, mobile, hardware) before one works with users.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **RewardMe** — Premature scaling killed it; it scaled before it had product-market fit.
- **GroupSpaces** — Committed premature scaling, building paid channels to prove numbers for fundraising.
- **Karhoo** — Built a scale-dependent transport service before it had any consumer reach.
- **MyFavorites** — Built its web and mobile apps at once before nailing the core process.

**Counter-move.**
- Define the retention and unit-economics gate the model must pass, and do not spend to scale before it does.
- Grow one market to proof before funding the next.
- Fund expansion from proven cashflow or a milestone the evidence already supports.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] What evidence proves the model works at current scale, and do I have it?
- [ ] Am I expanding to hit a number, or because the numbers already work?
- [ ] Would twice the scale make the flaws smaller or larger?

## C4. The Law of Operational Reality

> The business must survive its own operations; complexity, logistics, and execution failures kill as surely as a bad market.

**Why it holds.** Sprig found "the complexity of owning meal production through delivery at scale" overwhelming; Dinner Lab described "a really challenging logistical marketplace" where it was "always screwing up" with inconsistent margins; mass-customized jeans was overwhelmed by orders it processed nights and weekends; Tink Labs pursued growth at all costs until the organization unraveled. Modsy stopped serving customers without warning; Bolt Mobility vanished from cities, abandoning equipment. Operations are where promises meet physics, and companies that promise more than their operations can deliver die of the gap.

**Warning signs.**
- Negative operations margin that worsens as volume grows.
- Recurring quality, delivery, or support incidents with root causes unfixed.
- Operations headcount growing faster than the systems that should replace it.
- Promises (SLAs, delivery times, refunds) the operation cannot consistently meet.
- Growth pursued while the existing operation is still failing.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Sprig** — Owning meal production through delivery at scale proved too complex.
- **Dinner Lab** — Ran a logistically complex business with inconsistent margins.
- **Mass-customized Jeans** — Orders overwhelmed a nights-and-weekends operation.
- **Tink Labs** — Pursued growth too aggressively and the organization unraveled.
- **Modsy** — Stopped serving customers without warning, leaving refunds unsettled.
- **Bolt Mobility** — Vanished from cities, abandoning equipment and unpaid obligations.

**Counter-move.**
- Model operational capacity and cost per unit honestly, and simplify before scaling.
- Fix the recurring failure before adding volume that will multiply it.
- Exit a line of business cleanly and on time; a chaotic shutdown is its own failure mode.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Does the operation deliver its promise reliably at today's volume?
- [ ] Does my cost per unit improve or worsen as volume grows?
- [ ] Am I scaling a process that is still failing?

## C5. The Law of the Team

> A company is its people; founder conflict, bad hires, and burnout end companies the market would have spared.

**Why it holds.** Haven, a joint venture of three giants, failed because "each of the three founding companies executed their own projects separately" instead of the venture. Skully's founders were accused of spending investor funds on personal expenses; Planswell lost financing after a publicized sexual-harassment case; Cryptine Networks learned that "money comes between people" in founder equity disputes; EventVue's post-mortem listed cultural mistakes and compromised early hires; Monitor110 lacked a single "the buck stops here" leader until too late; Blurtt's founder burned out before the company could. Team failures are the most self-inflicted category in the source.

**Warning signs.**
- Unresolved founder conflict, unclear authority, or no single accountable leader.
- Key-person risk: the company cannot function if one person leaves.
- Hiring for agreement or expediency over talent and competence.
- Ethical, harassment, or conduct issues anywhere near leadership.
- Founder burnout, disengagement, or personal spending from company funds.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Haven** — Its three corporate parents ran their own projects instead of the joint venture.
- **Skully Helmets** — Founders were accused of spending investor funds on personal expenses.
- **Planswell** — A harassment case destroyed its financing and culture.
- **Cryptine Networks** — Founder equity disputes; money came between people.
- **EventVue** — Cited cultural mistakes, poor early hires, and slow learning.
- **Monitor110** — Lacked a single accountable leader until too late in the game.

**Counter-move.**
- Establish one accountable leader and clear decision rights, and vest equity with realistic terms from day one.
- Hire for the gap, check references, and fire fast on conduct — culture is a business asset.
- Watch founder energy as a leading indicator; burnout is a company risk, not a personal one.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Is there one person accountable for the outcome, and does everyone know who?
- [ ] Are founder roles and equity resolved and documented?
- [ ] Would the company survive the departure of any single person, including me?

## C6. The Law of Regulatory Reality

> A business that ignores the regulator, the courts, or the rule it depends on is one ruling away from closure — compliance is a cost of the model, not overhead.

**Why it holds.** LendUp was forced to stop lending after a CFPB action over misleading marketing; Singulex paid a fine and closed after a whistleblower suit; Binance left Singapore under pressure from the Monetary Authority; VidAngel was enjoined by a court; CanadaDrugs.com was indicted over misleading drug claims; Cambridge Analytica became insolvent after the data scandal; Homejoy's worker-classification lawsuits made fundraising much harder. This category is invisible in the growth model and decisive in the end, because a regulator or a court can switch off a revenue line overnight.

**Warning signs.**
- Operating in a grey zone, or counting on regulation that has not yet been written.
- Pending legislation or litigation that touches the core model.
- Data, privacy, safety, or classification practices that have not been reviewed.
- Platform or supplier policies the business depends on and does not control.
- A single legal challenge that could stop the product.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **LendUp** — A CFPB action over misleading marketing forced it to stop lending.
- **Singulex** — A whistleblower suit over unnecessary tests led to a fine and closure.
- **Binance Singapore** — Regulatory pressure from the Monetary Authority forced its exit.
- **VidAngel** — A court injunction over copyrighted content shut it down.
- **CanadaDrugs.com** — FDA and DOJ action over misleading drug claims ended it.
- **Cambridge Analytica** — A data scandal drove it into insolvency.
- **Homejoy** — Worker-classification lawsuits made fundraising much harder.

**Counter-move.**
- Map the regulatory regime the model depends on and get counsel before the model, not after.
- Build compliance into the unit economics rather than treating it as a later cost.
- Keep a legal-contingency plan for the one ruling that would stop the product.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] What rule, ruling, or regulator could stop my core revenue line, and how likely is it?
- [ ] Is compliance costed into the model, or hoped away?
- [ ] What is my plan for the day a regulator or court says no?

## C7. The Law of Adaptation

> Markets change; the company must change faster than they do, or the market will change it for them.

**Why it holds.** Backchat's founders could not "adapt fast enough to changing market and product conditions" and closed once funding dried up; Imercive stuck with "the wrong strategy for too long" because it was hard to admit the idea was not working, and by the time it pivoted there was not enough capital left. Adaptation is not the same as pivoting constantly (that is C1); it is responding to evidence with a changed plan while there is still room to execute. A strategy held past its evidence is a slow decision to fail.

**Evidence strength.** Thin — only a small number of the source entries name adaptation directly as the cause, so use this law to frame the question rather than to claim a broad pattern.

**Warning signs.**
- Metrics decline and the plan does not change; the answer is always "execute better".
- The response to new evidence is a new tactic, never a new strategy.
- A pivot everyone agrees is needed is repeatedly deferred for lack of runway.
- The company is defending a decision because of sunk cost, not new data.
- A version of the strategy has been "about to work" for several quarters.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Backchat** — Could not adapt fast enough to changing market and product conditions.
- **Imercive** — Stuck with the wrong strategy too long and pivoted only when capital was nearly gone.

**Counter-move.**
- Set explicit kill and iterate criteria for each strategy, and review them on a fixed cadence.
- Pivot from a position of capital, not from the bottom of the runway; budget enough to execute the new plan.
- Separate "execution is weak" from "the thesis is wrong", and test the thesis on evidence.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] What evidence would tell me the current strategy is wrong, and what would I do about it?
- [ ] Is my plan changing with the data, or am I defending it because of what I have already spent?
- [ ] If I had to pivot, would I still have the capital to execute it?

# Capital & Economics — The Laws

The seven laws in this file govern whether the money lasts and whether each unit of the business makes sense. They explain the largest single cluster of failures in the source: companies that had a market and could not survive their own economics or their own financing. Run these whenever a business is burning cash, losing money per order, dependent on someone else's money or platform, or deciding between raising and cutting. A company can violate every market law and still survive a while on capital; it cannot survive these.

## B1. The Law of Runway

> Runway is the one resource that cannot be replaced on short notice; when it ends, the company ends, whatever its traction.

**Why it holds.** Most entries in this index reach the same last sentence: the money ran out before the next milestone. Wow Air "ran out of time" to secure funding; Chef'd shut down immediately when financing fell through; Patterbuzz put it plainly, "We ran out of money." Vrideo was outfunded three to fifty times over and stretched a modest raise until it snapped; 1520 closed months after its seed round. Runway does not negotiate. A company with good economics and six weeks of cash dies sooner than a mediocre one with eighteen months.

**Warning signs.**
- Less than six months of cash, with no signed or confidently closing round.
- The next milestone is "raise the next round", and the round is not committed.
- A bridge followed by another bridge, each with worse terms.
- Revenue is growing but slower than burn.
- Hiring and fixed costs are set by the plan, not by cash on hand.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Wow Air** — Ran out of time and could not secure additional funding.
- **Chef'd** — Ceased operations the day its financing fell through.
- **Patterbuzz** — Never had enough money in the bank and ran out.
- **Vrideo** — Outfunded by rivals and exhausted its modest raise.
- **1520** — Shut down months after raising its seed round.

**Counter-move.**
- Hold at least twelve to eighteen months of runway at all times, and model the downside case, not the base case.
- Raise before you need to, from a position of strength, and never let the round become the milestone.
- Cut to a survivable cost base the moment the downside case triggers, not when the money is gone.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] At current burn, how many months of runway do I have, and what is the downside number?
- [ ] Is my next milestone something I can hit without raising, or is it "raise"?
- [ ] If the round fails entirely, what is my plan and when do I start it?

## B2. The Law of Unit Economics

> Growth that loses money on every unit is a countdown, not a flywheel — scale multiplies the model you actually have.

**Why it holds.** Fast's burn "far exceeded its revenue growth" and closed its fundraising window; Fridge No More's investors balked at "bad order economics"; Kettlebell Kitchen lost $12M a year; Miaoshenghou's fresh-food margins of 10–20% could not cover store rents. Juno reportedly lost about $1M a day, and the parent shut it down. Each of these businesses could point to orders, users, or cities, and each died because the arithmetic per unit never worked. Growth is only good news when each additional unit improves the position; otherwise it accelerates the end.

**Warning signs.**
- Contribution margin per order, job, or seat is negative, or positive only with a discount removed.
- Customer acquisition cost exceeds lifetime value.
- The plan says "scale will fix margins" with no line item that actually improves.
- Revenue grows in lockstep with losses.
- Investors cite "order economics" or "burn multiple" as the concern.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Fast** — Burn far exceeded revenue growth, closing its fundraising window.
- **Fridge No More** — Investors were concerned about bad order economics and the deal fell through.
- **Kettlebell Kitchen** — Lost $12M a year in a crowded meal-kit market.
- **Miaoshenghou** — Fresh-food margins of 10–20% could not cover its store rents.
- **Juno** — Lost roughly $1M a day and was shut down by its parent, Gett.

**Counter-move.**
- Prove positive contribution margin on a single unit before adding the next one, and publish the number internally.
- Tie every discount and promotion to a payback period, not to growth for its own sake.
- Model the fully-loaded cost (support, returns, delivery, churn) in the unit, not just the sale.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Do I make money on one unit, fully loaded, before any scale assumption?
- [ ] Is CAC below LTV with a payback I can actually finance?
- [ ] Which specific line improves as I scale, and by how much?

## B3. The Law of External Dependence

> A business whose survival rests on a single partner, platform, supplier, or patron is not fully owned by its founders, and can be ended by a decision it does not control.

**Why it holds.** Nuri shut down after one of its main business partners became insolvent; TenX's cards died when its issuer Wirecard filed for insolvency; Lookery exposed itself to "a huge single point of failure called Facebook". Nirvanix's lesson was the absence of due diligence and "reliance on any one vendor"; Yeloha could not get third-party project finance to install its systems; Plain Vanilla Games placed "too many eggs in the NBC basket." Dependence is invisible while it holds and fatal the moment it breaks.

**Warning signs.**
- One platform, supplier, or partner accounts for a large share of users, revenue, or supply.
- A commercial or contractual relationship you do not control sits between you and your customer.
- No tested second source, fallback, or exit clause.
- Your economics depend on another party's pricing or goodwill.
- A single patron (investor, corporate parent, celebrity) is the reason the company exists.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Nuri** — Closed after one of its main business partners became insolvent.
- **TenX** — Discontinued its cards when issuer Wirecard filed for insolvency.
- **Lookery** — Exposed itself to a huge single point of failure called Facebook.
- **Nirvanix** — Failed through reliance on unreliable vendors and no due diligence.
- **Yeloha** — Could not convince third-party project financiers to back its model.
- **Plain Vanilla Games** — Placed too many eggs in the NBC basket and the show fell through.

**Counter-move.**
- List every external dependency that could end the company, and for each define a fallback and a trigger.
- Negotiate exit clauses and keep a second source live, even if unused.
- Never let a single partner own your customer relationship or your supply.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] What one relationship, if it ended tomorrow, would end the company?
- [ ] Do I have a tested fallback for every dependency on that list?
- [ ] Am I building my business on someone else's land?

## B4. The Law of the Mismatched Backer

> Capital carries expectations; when the backer's goal or timeline diverges from the company's, the backer ends it — the wrong money is worse than less money.

**Why it holds.** Argo AI was cut loose when Ford and Volkswagen shifted strategic focus; Backplane's founder said tough liquidation preferences scared off new investors and made a good exit impossible. Beepi "may have raised too much, too soon" and pushed for higher valuations until its exits broke down; Standout Jobs raised "too much money, too early" without the validation to justify it; Tigerbow learned not to raise from people who do not invest in startups; Bridj bet on a corporate deal instead of a venture round and the close date slid away. Each failure was not a shortage of money but a mismatch in what the money wanted.

**Warning signs.**
- A strategic or corporate investor whose own roadmap competes with yours.
- Terms — participating preferences, cumulative dividends, redemption rights — that block a future raise or ordinary sale.
- Investors unfamiliar with startup risk who expect near-term returns.
- Raising more than the plan needs, which inflates expectations and dilutes focus.
- A single backer whose exit is the company's only exit.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Argo AI** — Its corporate backers, Ford and VW, shifted focus and cut it loose.
- **Backplane** — Tough liquidation preferences from big-name investors scared off new money.
- **Beepi** — Raised too much too soon and pushed valuations until its exit talks collapsed.
- **Standout Jobs** — Raised too much money too early without the validation to justify it.
- **Tigerbow** — Raised from friends and family who do not invest in startups.
- **Bridj** — Bet on a corporate deal instead of a venture round, and the deal never closed.

**Counter-move.**
- Choose backers whose time horizon and objectives match the business, and check references with their founders.
- Read the terms that govern a bad outcome, not just the headline valuation.
- Raise the amount the plan needs, not the amount you can get.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Do my investors' goals and timeline match the business plan, or diverge from it?
- [ ] Do my terms let me raise again and sell normally if things go well?
- [ ] Am I raising to hit a number, or because the plan requires it?

## B5. The Law of Revenue Before Fundraising

> A business that must raise to survive is not yet a business; revenue is the only proof investors cannot reinterpret.

**Why it holds.** The founder of Link Management System reduced his failure to a single line: "We didn't sell anything", four times over. Omniref "was never able to find a sustainable business model" that justified the cost of running the site; Fieldbook was "never profitable" and could not grow revenue fast enough; Stipple turned on revenue but could not make dollars "line up with our expense structure"; Melotic found "not enough growth to justify the ongoing costs." Fundraising on a plan is betting that a stranger will believe you; selling to a customer is proof. The cases that raised before revenue often died before revenue arrived.

**Warning signs.**
- No paying customer, or revenue far below the cost of serving.
- The pitch deck's core claim is a market and a plan rather than a paying cohort.
- Retention, repeat purchase, or expansion is unmeasured.
- "We'll raise, then figure out the revenue model."
- Growth is flat and the answer is always another round.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Link Management System** — Failed because it did not sell anything, by its own account.
- **Omniref** — Never found a sustainable business model that justified the site's expense.
- **Fieldbook** — Was never profitable and could not grow revenue fast enough.
- **Stipple** — Turned on revenue but could not align dollars with its expense structure.
- **Melotic** — Did not experience enough growth to justify ongoing development costs.
- **Campus** — Could not make the business economically viable despite several model changes.

**Counter-move.**
- Sell before you build the full product; a paid commitment is the only true validation.
- Run the company against revenue, not against the next round.
- Treat every fundraise as an accelerant for a working model, never as the model itself.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Do I have paying customers whose revenue is growing without a new round?
- [ ] Can I show a cohort that retains, repeats, or expands?
- [ ] Would I still have a business if I never raised again?

## B6. The Law of Cost Discipline

> Every dollar of avoidable cost is a day of runway burned; overspending is invisible until the raise fails.

**Why it holds.** Zirtual's post-mortem named the cause outright: burn, "the amount of money that goes out the door, over and above what comes in." DAQRI burned more than $250M and acquired four companies before folding; Kaodim blamed rising costs as much as the pandemic. Sprawl in headcount, offices, hardware, or acquisitions outruns the revenue that is supposed to justify it, and by the time the gap shows, the runway is gone. Frugality is not a virtue in itself, but uncontrolled cost removes the option to wait for the market.

**Evidence strength.** Thin — the source states direct cost-discipline failures less often than it states revenue or fundraising failures, so read this law together with B1 and B2 rather than on its own.

**Warning signs.**
- Headcount and fixed costs grow faster than revenue or retention.
- Heavy capital or real-estate commitments before product-market fit.
- Spend is justified by "the plan" rather than by a live metric.
- No one owns the burn number, or it is reported only to the board.
- The company cannot name what it would cut first.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Zirtual** — Died of burn: it spent more than it earned.
- **DAQRI** — Burned more than $250M and acquired four entities before folding.
- **Kaodim** — Cited rising costs, along with pandemic disruption, as a driver of closure.
- **Saaspire** — Did not stabilize its bootstrapped cashflow before spending on product.
- **Findory** — Learned that "a cheap is good, but too cheap is bad", and starved itself of what it needed.

**Counter-move.**
- Tie every hire and every fixed cost to a metric it must move, and review the burn number weekly.
- Keep a written cut list ready so reductions are fast and non-political when they are needed.
- Spend on growth only from a position where the unit economics already work.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Is the burn number owned, reported, and tied to a plan I can cut?
- [ ] Does every fixed cost earn its place against a live metric?
- [ ] If revenue stopped growing, how many weeks until I would have to act?

## B7. The Law of the Downward Spiral

> Financial distress is a spiral: a failed raise forces cuts, the cuts reduce the traction needed to raise, and each turn is faster than the last.

**Why it holds.** ChaCha's advertising revenue collapsed, its lender emptied its accounts, and no suitor appeared; HubHaus missed payments for months and "imploded", stranding tenants and homeowners; Wonga could not return to profitability under redress claims; Katerra filed for bankruptcy amid mismanagement and a collapsed lender; Theranos ran out of time once its fraud was exposed. Distress is not a static state. Once a company misses a payment, the options narrow, the best people leave, and the next financing gets harder — which is why the spiral must be broken early, while there is still a choice.

**Warning signs.**
- Payroll, rent, or vendor payments are late or being negotiated.
- A lender or creditor takes action, or covenants are breached.
- The bridge round fails and the next one is smaller and worse.
- Senior people leave and cannot be replaced.
- The company is selling assets or "strategic alternatives" to survive.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **ChaCha** — Ad revenue collapsed, a secured lender emptied its accounts, and no buyer appeared.
- **HubHaus** — Imploded after months of missed payments, stranding renters and homeowners.
- **Wonga** — Could not be restored to profitability because of the level of redress claims.
- **Katerra** — Filed for bankruptcy amid mismanagement and a collapsed lender.
- **Theranos** — Was "now out of time" once its fraud was exposed.
- **Yunniao** — Competition and the pandemic set it into a downward spiral, strapped for cash.

**Counter-move.**
- Treat the first missed payment as a crisis trigger: cut to cash-positive, renegotiate, and go to the board immediately.
- Restructure while still solvent, when you still control the terms.
- Preserve a crisis reserve and know the difference between a bridge and a death spiral.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Are all obligations current, and if not, what is the recovery plan and its deadline?
- [ ] Is the next raise bigger and cheaper, or smaller and worse?
- [ ] Am I restructuring from control, or waiting until a creditor decides for me?

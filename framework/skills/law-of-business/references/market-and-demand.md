# Market & Demand — The Laws

The seven laws in this file govern whether there is a business at all: a real problem, a market big and timely enough to pay for a solution, a customer able and willing to pay, a route to reach them, and a reason to choose you over the alternative. A company can be well run, well funded, and well staffed and still die here, because no amount of execution fixes a market that will not pay. Read these first when the question is "is this worth building?"; the capital and execution laws only matter once the answer is yes.

## A1. The Law of a Real Problem

> A business dies when it solves a problem customers do not actually have — or do not have badly enough to change what they do.

**Why it holds.** The post-mortems are full of founders who shipped a working product and found no one waiting for it. Treehouse Logic had "great technology, great data, great advisors" but was "not solving a large enough problem"; Patient Communicator found doctors wanted more patients, not a more efficient office; Dinnr calls the absence of a real opportunity "the original sin" that no later iteration could fix. When the problem is real, customers pull the product into use; when it is invented, every push feels like pushing a rope.

**Warning signs.**
- Signups spike after launch, then churn; nothing pulls users back.
- You can describe the product but not the person who feels the pain daily.
- Interviews produce "that's neat" or "I'd use it", not "when can I have it?".
- Adoption depends on convincing users to change a habit they like; Chorus lost users to hard-wired behavior after eight months.
- Conversion of pilots and trials stalls with no single blocking objection.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Treehouse Logic** — Was not solving a large enough problem to serve with a scalable solution.
- **Patient Communicator** — Had no customers because no one wanted the model it pitched.
- **Hivebeat** — Never hit real product-market fit, and its product was not a 10x improvement.
- **Dinnr** — There never was an opportunity, so later iteration was "aspirin to a deathbed patient".
- **Pumodo** — The founders were not users of their own product and had fallen in love with the idea.

**Counter-move.**
- Before building, get three customers to describe the problem unprompted, and get one to pay or sign a letter of intent.
- Require a paid pilot or real commitment before the next build cycle; a compliment is not a signal.
- Kill or re-scope any feature no customer asked for and no customer uses twice.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Can I name three specific people who have this problem badly enough to pay for it today?
- [ ] Have I watched a customer work around the problem, rather than just say they would use my fix?
- [ ] If I stopped all marketing, would any current user come back on their own?

## A2. The Law of Market Size

> A market too small to carry the cost structure of the business is a ceiling, not a startup — the math has to work at attack scale, not just at pilot scale.

**Why it holds.** Several failures were real products for real problems that were simply too small to ever fund the company built around them. Imzy built a kinder Reddit and "was not able to find our place in the market"; Kitchensurfing never produced enough demand in either of its models to sustain a venture-backed business; BlackJet needed "critical mass" and never reached it. Besomebody put the number plainly: the model only worked if tens of millions of people booked one or two experiences a year. A niche can be a great lifestyle business and a fatal venture business at the same time.

**Warning signs.**
- The plan requires "just 1%" of a huge market, or a behavior change at implausible scale.
- Fixed costs (engineering, compliance, real estate, hardware) exceed what the addressable base can pay.
- Early customers are enthusiastic but few, and each new one is harder to find.
- Reachable market is counted top-down (TAM×rate) rather than bottom-up (named accounts × price).
- The product only works if it becomes a habit across millions of people simultaneously.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Imzy** — Could not find its place in the market despite a loyal niche.
- **Kitchensurfing** — Neither its booking nor its on-demand model produced enough demand to be sustainable.
- **BlackJet** — Was a critical-mass business that never reached critical mass.
- **Besomebody** — Needed tens of millions of repeat bookers, and the demand simply was not there.
- **Markafoni** — Was not scaling sufficiently to be sustainable in its market.

**Counter-move.**
- Build TAM bottom-up: named customers × realistic annual price × reachable share, and show the fixed-cost line it must clear.
- Test whether the beachhead segment can alone fund the company; if not, prove an adjacent segment before raising.
- Prefer a small market you can dominate and expand from over a huge one you cannot reach.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Does the bottom-up market size clear the company's fixed costs without a "1% of China" assumption?
- [ ] Can the first segment alone produce a viable business if no second segment ever arrives?
- [ ] Is growth coming from a finite list of real buyers, or from a hope about mass behavior?

## A3. The Law of Timing

> The right product at the wrong moment fails exactly like the wrong product — market timing is a variable, not a footnote.

**Why it holds.** Starsky Robotics' CEO wrote that "timing, more than anything else, is what I think is to blame," because the autonomous-trucking space was overwhelmed and underfunded. Calxeda was "too early," shipping 32-bit chips when customers wanted 64-bit and the operating-system ecosystem was still forming. On-Q-ity got the technology right and "the market-timing wrong" and could not fund the gap; Airware mis-timed the commercial-drone transition; Seismic Video was pre-iPhone. The same pattern runs through demand shocks: CommonBond's loan pause, Brideside's wedding shutdowns, and Stockwell's office vending all died when the world moved under them.

**Warning signs.**
- Customers are interested but budget cycles, standards, or infrastructure are not ready.
- A complementary technology or regulation your product needs is still maturing.
- Demand is a fad that peaked before you shipped, or a shock has moved it.
- You are educating the whole market rather than satisfying it.
- The plan requires a platform shift that is "coming soon".

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Starsky Robotics** — Its CEO blamed timing: the space was overwhelmed and underfunded.
- **Calxeda** — Moved faster than its customers and shipped 32-bit silicon when the market wanted 64-bit.
- **On-Q-ity** — Got the technology right but the market timing wrong, and could not fund the wait.
- **CommonBond** — The pandemic payment pause destroyed its refinance market.
- **Seismic Video** — Launched before iPhone and Android made its approach viable.

**Counter-move.**
- Distinguish "not yet" from "never": find the leading indicator that tells you which one this is.
- Size the runway needed to survive until the market is ready, and decide consciously whether to wait or pivot.
- Build for the market that exists today; let the early market fund the bet on the one that is coming.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Is demand blocked by a concrete precondition (standard, device, rule, budget), and when does it clear?
- [ ] Can I name the leading indicator I would watch to know the market is arriving?
- [ ] If the market arrives two years late, does the company still exist to meet it?

## A4. The Law of Willingness to Pay

> Users liking a product is not the same as users paying for it; if they will not pay more than it costs to serve them, it is a hobby, not a business.

**Why it holds.** Juicero couldn't build a manufacturing and distribution system that made economic sense; Shoes of Prey learned "the hard way that mass market customers don't want to create, they want to be inspired"; RealTime Worlds made a virtue of having no business model at all. Quibi raised $1.75B and never found an audience willing to pay for short-form mobile video, and PlayCafe found that content is "an order of magnitude harder than technology with an order less upside." The gap between "I like this" and "I'll pay enough for this" is where a large share of these companies died.

**Warning signs.**
- The plan says "we'll figure out monetization after we scale".
- Free or freemium users show no path to conversion at a price above cost.
- The business depends on advertising, which needs an audience the company cannot realistically assemble.
- Customers use the product but resist the price, the bundle, or the subscription.
- The company prices by its own effort rather than by the value delivered.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Shoes of Prey** — Found mass-market customers did not want to design their own shoes.
- **Quibi** — Raised $1.75B but could not buy traction for short-form mobile video.
- **RealTime Worlds** — Built its game with no business model, which its founder called a recipe for failure.
- **Poliana** — Found it very hard to make money on something that ought to be free.
- **Delight** — Learned customers pay for information, not raw data, and its service never made them smarter.
- **Travelllll.com** — Had an advertising model but not the enormous audience advertising requires.

**Counter-move.**
- Charge from day one, even at a small price, to test willingness to pay rather than interest.
- Price against the value delivered and the cost to serve, not against the effort spent.
- Treat any business that only works at scale as one that must be funded like a research bet.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Will a target customer pay a price today that exceeds the fully-loaded cost to serve them?
- [ ] If funding stopped, would paying customers alone keep the lights on?
- [ ] Is my monetization a real model, or a promise to "figure it out later"?

## A5. The Law of Distribution

> A product nobody can find, or keep, is not a business — distribution is a durable capability, not a launch event.

**Why it holds.** Tutorspree "didn't scale because we were single channel dependent" on SEO, and that channel shifted radically and suddenly. Vidme's creators could not move audiences off YouTube and Facebook, so the platform stayed empty. Everpix spent too long on product and "began marketing too late"; Kinly postponed "the distribution challenge" and died of it; Overto hit the ceiling of effortless growth with no one skilled in marketing. Distribution is not a spike you buy at launch; it is the repeatable engine that turns a good product into a company.

**Warning signs.**
- One channel drives most acquisition, and its cost or rules are outside your control.
- Growth stalls immediately after the launch spike, with no second engine.
- A platform you do not own sits between you and your users.
- You believe a better product will market itself.
- CAC is rising faster than retention improves.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Tutorspree** — Was single-channel dependent on SEO, and that channel shifted against it.
- **Vidme** — Creators could not transition audiences from YouTube and Facebook.
- **Everpix** — Spent too much on product and not enough on growth, and marketed too late.
- **Kinly** — Postponed the distribution challenge until it killed the product.
- **Overto** — Hit the ceiling of effortless growth with nobody skilled in marketing.

**Counter-move.**
- Prove one repeatable, owned acquisition channel with acceptable economics before scaling spend.
- Build a direct relationship with users — email, community, content — so no platform can switch you off.
- Set a second channel in motion before the first saturates.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Can I state the one channel that reliably acquires customers below their lifetime value?
- [ ] Do I own the relationship with my users, or does a platform I do not control own it?
- [ ] What is my plan for the day my main channel's rules or cost change?

## A6. The Law of Differentiation

> If customers cannot tell you apart from the cheaper alternative, they will choose the cheaper alternative — a "better" version of an incumbent is not a business.

**Why it holds.** 8tracks lost its listeners to Spotify; Zen99 had found a user-acquisition path until "Intuit released an identical competing product"; Kato died because "Slack ate the world"; Picturelife was displaced by Google Photos and Amazon. Baroo was outgunned by better-funded rivals serving more cities, and Wesabe lost to Mint because it asked for more work to get a worse experience. Competing on features an incumbent can copy, against capital you do not have, is a losing position dressed up as a product strategy.

**Warning signs.**
- The pitch is "like X, but better / cheaper / nicer".
- A well-funded incumbent ships your core feature, or bundles it for free.
- Customers see no switching cost or reason to leave the default.
- The differentiator is something money can buy (design, speed, price) rather than something structural.
- Sales conversations end in price negotiation rather than value.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **8tracks** — Lost listeners to Spotify as a bigger player satisfied them better.
- **Zen99** — Was killed when Intuit released an identical competing product.
- **Kato** — Found no traction because "Slack ate the world".
- **Picturelife** — Was displaced by Google Photos and Amazon in cloud photo storage.
- **Wesabe** — Lost to Mint because it demanded more work for a worse experience.
- **Baroo** — Was outcompeted by better-funded pet-care rivals.

**Counter-move.**
- Name the one thing you do that an incumbent structurally cannot or will not copy, and test it with customers.
- Choose a wedge the incumbent must ignore to protect its core economics.
- Treat "better" as a temporary advantage; build switching costs, data, or network effects around it.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] In one sentence, why would a customer choose me over the cheaper, well-funded alternative?
- [ ] Would my differentiator survive the incumbent bundling a copy of it for free?
- [ ] Is the advantage structural (data, network, switching cost) or merely a feature?

## A7. The Law of the Right Customer

> The same product sold to the wrong segment fails — the customer must be the one who feels the pain and holds the budget.

**Why it holds.** Shyp "didn't listen" to warnings that chasing consumers was wrong, and consumers ship too rarely to build a business on. VoterTide shipped features it thought were great without gathering client input; HelloParking "rarely got out of the building" and never defined hypotheses or spoke to target users. Unifyo built for end-users and SMEs while the paying market had moved to the enterprise. The product can be right while the buyer is wrong, and the buyer is the one who decides whether the company lives.

**Evidence strength.** Thin — only a handful of the source entries state the wrong-customer mechanism directly, so treat this law as directional and stress-test it with primary research rather than by pattern-matching the index.

**Warning signs.**
- The user and the person who signs the cheque are different people with different needs.
- Sales cycles run far longer than the company can absorb.
- The segment you designed for does not control a budget.
- "We'll start with consumers and move upmarket" with no evidence the enterprise will buy.
- Customers are enthusiastic but never the ones who buy.

**In the post-mortems.**
- **Shyp** — Chased consumers who rarely ship and ignored warnings that it was the wrong strategy.
- **VoterTide** — Rolled out features it believed in without gathering client input.
- **HelloParking** — Rarely got out of the building and rarely talked to target end-users.
- **Unifyo** — Could not empathize with large corporations and targeted the wrong end of the market.
- **MobileIgniter** — Found its target segment's sales cycles too long for a small company to absorb.

**Counter-move.**
- Write down the buyer, the budget they control, and the trigger that makes them spend — and interview ten of them.
- Sell only to the segment that feels the pain and can pay, even if a bigger segment looks more attractive.
- If the user is not the buyer, design for the buyer's constraints from the start.

**Check yourself.**
- [ ] Do I know exactly who signs the cheque, and have I spoken to at least ten of them?
- [ ] Does my target segment control a budget and feel the pain directly?
- [ ] Am I selling to the user I like, rather than the buyer who pays?
